## 1. Fluid (Gf) vs. crystallized (Gc) intelligence, CHC, lifespan trajectories, and Ackerman's PPIK

### Takeaway
Psychometrics has formally separated "reasoning with novel material" (Gf) from "acquired knowledge" (Gc) since Cattell and Horn, and the two follow different lifespan curves. Gf-type abilities (reasoning, speed, working memory) peak around the early-to-mid 20s and then decline. Vocabulary and general knowledge keep rising until at least about 60, and vocabulary peaks around the late 60s to early 70s. The two are still positively correlated, though the correlation is weaker in old age. Ackerman's PPIK theory goes further: in adulthood, "intelligence-as-knowledge" becomes the main expression of intellect, so by a knowledge criterion middle-aged adults may count as more intelligent than 18-year-olds.

### Cited Findings
**Founding study (Horn & Cattell, 1967)**
- Horn, J. L., & Cattell, R. B. (1967). "Age differences in fluid and crystallized intelligence." *Acta Psychologica*, 26, 107–129. DOI: 10.1016/0001-6918(67)90011-X — [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Age-differences-in-fluid-and-crystallized-Horn-Cattell/6d76c8653314b5cce47d8d6ccc159fb07c148143)
- A secondary summary says the study combined a test battery into Gf and Gc estimates for 297 participants in five age groups spanning 14–61 years. Fluid ability rose through adolescence, leveled off in young adulthood, then declined. Crystallized ability kept rising across the age range (secondary; verify against the original) — [Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/fluid-crystallized-intelligence.html)

**Gf–Gc correlation by age**
- Cunningham, Clayton & Overton (1975), "Fluid and crystallized intelligence in young adulthood and old age" (*Journal of Gerontology*). The Gf–Gc correlation was significantly lower in the elderly sample (r = .386) than among young adults (r = .672). The two constructs diverge with age — [ResearchGate abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/22046779_Fluid_and_Crystallized_Intelligence_in_Young_Adulthood_and_Old_Age)

**Salthouse (2009)**
- Salthouse, T. A. (2009). "When does age-related cognitive decline begin?" *Neurobiology of Aging*, 30(4), 507–514 — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/24028888_When_does_age-related_cognitive_decline_begin)
  - Top performance on some tests came at age 22.
  - Declines in abstract reasoning, processing speed and puzzle-solving were notable by age 27.
  - Average memory declines were detectable by about age 37.
  - "Accumulated knowledge skills, such as improvement of vocabulary and general knowledge, actually increase at least until the age of 60" — [EurekAlert press release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/849530)
- **Contested:** Schaie's commentary argues that Salthouse "again reifies the cross-sectional fallacy", meaning cross-sectional data cannot date the onset of individual decline — [PMC2680669](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2680669/). A second commentary, "Challenging the notion of an early-onset of cognitive decline", says longitudinal data put the onset of decline much later, around 60 — [PubMed 19285194](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19285194/)

**Hartshorne & Germine (2015)**
- Hartshorne, J. K., & Germine, L. T. (2015). "When does cognitive functioning peak? The asynchronous rise and fall of different cognitive abilities across the life span." *Psychological Science* — [PMC4441622](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4441622/)
  - Data came from large online samples (gameswithwords.org, testmybrain.org) and were checked against older paper-and-pencil norms, which reproduced the same peaks.
  - Most measures, such as memory and pattern recognition/processing speed, peaked from the late teens into the 20s.
  - Math, general knowledge and comprehension kept improving into adulthood. Vocabulary peaked latest, in the late 60s to early 70s (secondary press coverage) — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/different-mental-abilities-peak-different-times-life-18-70); [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2015/03/smarter-by-the-minute-sort-of/)
  - Hartshorne: "there's probably not one age at which you're peak on most things, much less all of them."
  - Germine suggested that the later peaks of knowledge skills partly reflect improvements in education and the rise of white-collar work — [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2015/03/smarter-by-the-minute-sort-of/); [Big Think](https://bigthink.com/neuropsych/at-what-age-are-you-the-smartest/)

**Ackerman's PPIK theory**
- Ackerman, P. L. (1996). "A theory of adult intellectual development: Process, personality, interests, and knowledge." *Intelligence*, 22(2), 227–257 — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0160289696900161); [ERIC EJ536958](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ536958)
  - PPIK combines four elements: intelligence-as-process (basic reasoning and memory, roughly Gf), personality, interests, and intelligence-as-knowledge.
  - It describes a developmental progression "from process to knowledge." Because reasoning and memory decline with normal aging, knowledge from educational, vocational and avocational domains becomes central to adult intelligence — [ERIC full text, Ackerman "Knowledge Structures and Adult Intellectual Development"](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED562744.pdf)
- Ackerman, P. L. (2000). "Domain-specific knowledge as the 'dark matter' of adult intelligence: Gf/Gc, personality and interest correlates." *Journals of Gerontology: Psychological Sciences*, 55(2), P69 — [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/psychsocgerontology/article/55/2/P69/578710); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/12523575_Domain-Specific_Knowledge_as_the_Dark_Matter_of_Adult_Intelligence_GfGc_Personality_and_Interest_Correlates)
  - Studies in this program found positive age–knowledge relations across many academic and non-academic domains, such as current events, finances, health and technology (secondary summary) — [ERIC full text](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED562744.pdf)
- Ackerman's argument, from an address rather than a single study: "if credit is given for what people 'know'… middle-aged adults might be more intelligent (or at least as intelligent), compared to 18-year-olds" — [APS Observer, "Intelligence Redefined"](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/intelligence-redefined)

**How CHC frames Gc**
- In later work the CHC factor Gc is usually described as "comprehension-knowledge." For example, Sala et al. list chess-related abilities as "fluid intelligence, processing speed, short-term and working memory, and comprehension knowledge" — [PMC5322219](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5322219/)
- Kan, Kievit, Dolan & van der Maas (2011) show that the CHC Gc factor is statistically equivalent to verbal comprehension and that Gf and g cannot be told apart. See Q6 — [VU Amsterdam](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/on-the-interpretation-of-the-chc-factor-gc/)

### Inferences
- The Gf/Gc dissociation over the lifespan is the strongest single psychometric argument for "conhecimento ≠ inteligência." A 70-year-old can have more knowledge (higher Gc, larger vocabulary) and less reasoning-on-novelty (lower Gf) than a 25-year-old. Knowledge and reasoning are therefore separable quantities that can move in opposite directions within one person.
- The same evidence also shows the two are correlated, especially in youth (r ≈ .67 in young adults vs. .39 in the elderly in one classic study). The honest claim is "distinct but related," not "independent."
- Ackerman complicates the thesis from the other side. If "intelligence" means what adults use to function in real domains, knowledge may be most of it. The thesis needs to define "intelligence" as process/reasoning for the slogan to hold.
- The Salthouse "peak at 22" figure is cross-sectional and disputed. The thesis should present it as "cross-sectional data suggest…" and pair it with the longitudinal critique.

### Gaps
- I could not open Horn & Cattell (1967) to extract its own Gf–Gc correlations or exact age curves. The 297-participant / 14–61 detail is secondary.
- Hartshorne & Germine's exact peak ages per test (e.g., digit-symbol around 18–19, working memory in the mid-20s to 30s, face memory in the early 30s, emotion recognition in the 40s–50s) are from my memory of the paper and were not confirmed this session. Verify in PMC4441622.
- I found no source this session for a "typical" modern Gf–Gc correlation in large CHC batteries. Values of about .5–.7 are commonly cited but unverified here.
- I did not retrieve McGrew's (2009) canonical CHC paper ("CHC theory and the human cognitive abilities project," *Intelligence*) or Cattell's (1963/1971/1987) primary texts on investment theory.

## 2. Expertise research: chunking, deliberate practice, domain-specificity, and (lack of) far transfer

### Takeaway
Chess masters' famous memory advantage comes from domain knowledge (tens of thousands of stored "chunks"), not general memory or reasoning capacity. The advantage nearly disappears on random positions: it is "small but reliable," and in Chase & Simon's first study the master did worse than the novice. Training a domain skill (chess, music, working-memory games) produces little or no far transfer to general cognitive ability once study quality is controlled. Expert knowledge is powerful and domain-bound. On the counter side, Gf and Gc both correlate positively with chess skill, and deliberate practice explains only about 12% of performance variance overall.

### Cited Findings
**Chunking and random positions**
- Chase, W. G., & Simon, H. A. (1973). "Perception in chess." *Cognitive Psychology*, 4(1), 55–81. Chase & Simon (1973) and Simon & Gilmartin (1973) estimated that masters store on the order of 50,000 chunks. Gobet & Simon (1996) say human data support that estimate — [ResearchGate, Gobet & Simon 1996, *Memory & Cognition*](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225336454_Recall_of_random_and_distorted_chess_positions_Implications_for_the_theory_of_expertise)
- Gobet, F., & Simon, H. A. (1996). "Recall of random and distorted chess positions: Implications for the theory of expertise." *Memory & Cognition*, 24, 493–503. Companion paper: "Recall of rapidly presented random chess positions is a function of skill," *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* (1996) — [Brunel repository](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/1346/1/FullText.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/258430646_Recall_of_rapidly_presented_random_chess_positions_is_a_function_of_skill)
  - Across reviewed experiments, stronger players recall more pieces even on random boards. The exception is Chase & Simon's (1973) first study, where the master did worse than the novice.
  - Chunking theory explains the residual advantage: masters are more likely to find familiar fragments even in random layouts. In simulations, recall rose logarithmically with the number of learned chunks — [Brunel](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/1346/1/FullText.pdf)
  - The skill advantage on random positions is "small but reliable," attributed to small familiar patterns that occur by chance — [Jackson, ICCM 2001](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/770/1/ICCM-2001-Jackson.pdf)
- Gobet & Simon later extended chunking into "template theory" (1998), in which frequently recurring chunks evolve into larger schematic structures — [Gobet & Simon 1998 PDF](http://www.chrest.info/Fribourg_Cours_Expertise/Articles-www/II%20Donnees%20empiriques/Gobet&Simon--PsycResearch--1998.pdf)

**Deliberate practice and its limits**
- Macnamara, B. N., Hambrick, D. Z., & Oswald, F. L. (2014). "Deliberate practice and performance in music, games, sports, education, and professions: A meta-analysis." *Psychological Science*. DOI: 10.1177/0956797614535810 — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797614535810); [author PDF](https://scottbarrykaufman.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/07/Macnamara-et-al.-2014.pdf)
  - Deliberate practice explained about 12% of performance variance overall (95% CI 9–15%, per the corrigendum table).
  - By domain: about 26% for games, 21% for music, 18% for sports, 4% for education, and 1% or less for professions (secondary summaries; verify against the article tables).
- Ericsson's group disputes this, arguing that Macnamara et al. used too broad a definition of deliberate practice (e.g., counting lectures and group coaching) — [Ericsson & Harwell response, PMC8049893](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8049893/). Others argue that the meta-analyses underestimate effects because they ignore individualization — [Springer, *Current Psychology* 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12144-021-02326-x)

**Does training transfer? (Sala & Gobet meta-analyses)**
- Sala, G., & Gobet, F. (2016). "Do the benefits of chess instruction transfer to academic and cognitive skills? A meta-analysis." *Educational Research Review*, 18, 46–57 — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1747938X16300112); [ChessBase summary](https://en.chessbase.com/post/chess-instruction-in-schools-a-quantitative-review)
  - Modest overall effect: g = 0.338 (k = 40 effect sizes from 24 studies, N = 5,221). Mathematics g = 0.382; reading g = 0.248. Longer training predicted larger effects.
  - The authors caution that no study had an "ideal design," so placebo effects cannot be ruled out.
- Sala, G., & Gobet, F. (2017). "Does far transfer exist? Negative evidence from chess, music, and working memory training." *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, 26(6), 515–520. DOI: 10.1177/0963721417712760 — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963721417712760); [LSE open access](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/102204/)
  - Three meta-analyses of children's studies on chess, music, and working-memory training. Overall effects were small to moderate, and effect sizes shrank as design quality rose (e.g., with active control groups). Far transfer "rarely occurs" — [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/no-far-transfer-chess-memory-training-and-music-just-make-you-better-chess-memory)
- "Near and far transfer in cognitive training: A second-order meta-analysis." *Collabra: Psychology*, 5(1), 18 (2019; Sala, Gobet and colleagues) — [UC Press](https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/5/1/18/113004/Near-and-Far-Transfer-in-Cognitive-Training-A)
  - Chess far transfer: ḡ = 0.13, 95% CI [−0.02, 0.27], k = 9. With active controls only: ḡ = 0.05 (k = 3).
  - Working-memory training shows near transfer, but far-transfer effects are small or null. Once placebo effects and publication bias are controlled, the overall far-transfer effect is about zero (secondary summary of the abstract).

**Counter-evidence: chess skill does correlate with intelligence**
- Burgoyne, A. P., Sala, G., Gobet, F., Macnamara, B. N., Campitelli, G., & Hambrick, D. Z. (2016). "The relationship between cognitive ability and chess skill: A comprehensive meta-analysis." *Intelligence*, 59, 72–83. DOI: 10.1016/j.intell.2016.08.002 — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0160289616301593); [LSE](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/102241/)
  - Gf, Gc, short-term memory and processing speed all correlated positively and significantly with chess skill. The Gf–chess link was moderated by age and skill level.
  - A secondary account says that among established adult players, especially experts above about 2000 Elo, intelligence does not seem to influence skill level (secondary; verify) — [Chessable blog](https://www.chessable.com/blog/chess-science-in-the-making/)

### Inferences
- Expertise research is very strong evidence that knowledge is not general intelligence. Superior expert performance is tied to the structure of the domain: shuffle the pieces and the advantage nearly vanishes. Training the domain does not make people generally smarter.
- The same literature shows that intelligence matters for acquiring expertise (positive Gf/Gc–chess correlations, especially in children and novices). Its influence may fade at high skill levels, where accumulated knowledge dominates. One thesis-ready phrasing: intelligence helps build knowledge, but knowledge, once built, does not turn back into general intelligence.
- The "10,000 hours" popular claim is undermined by the 12% variance finding. Neither knowledge from practice nor raw intelligence alone explains expert performance.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm Chase & Simon's (1973) exact recall figures from the full text. From memory, after 5-second exposures the master recalled about 16 pieces on game positions vs. about 8 for a class-A player and about 4 for a beginner, with all three near 2–3 pieces on random positions. These numbers are unverified this session.
- I did not retrieve de Groot's original work (*Thought and Choice in Chess*, 1946/1965). The commonly cited finding is that grandmasters reproduced briefly shown positions at about 90%+ accuracy vs. about 50% for weaker players, and did not search more moves than weaker players. Unverified here.
- I did not retrieve Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993), *Psychological Review* 100(3), 363–406, or Ericsson & Pool's *Peak* (2016). The often-quoted "about 10,000 hours by age 20 for top violinists" figure needs verification in the original.
- Exact correlation coefficients from Burgoyne et al. (2016), recalled as r ≈ .24 for Gf and ≈ .22 for Gc, were not confirmed.
- The full author list of the 2019 *Collabra* paper (recalled as Sala, Aksayli, Tatlidil, Tatsumi, Gondo & Gobet) was not confirmed by the search result.

## 3. Rationality vs. intelligence: Stanovich (dysrationalia, *What Intelligence Tests Miss*, CART) and the Cognitive Reflection Test

### Takeaway
Stanovich's program shows that IQ tests measure "algorithmic" capacity, not the tendency to use it well. Many thinking biases, myside bias in particular, are largely independent of cognitive ability. People with higher ability show an equal or even larger "bias blind spot." More numerate people can be more polarized on politically charged data. The CRT shows that resisting an intuitive wrong answer is partly separate from IQ (r ≈ .43–.44 with SAT and Wonderlic). Rationality and intelligence are still not orthogonal: the composite rational-thinking test correlates about .50–.70 with cognitive ability.

### Cited Findings
**Dysrationalia: definition and books**
- Stanovich coined "dysrationalia" in 1993: "the inability to think and behave rationally despite adequate intelligence." His diagnostic criterion is a level of rationality "significantly below the level of the individual's intellectual capacity (as determined by an individually administered IQ test)" — [Wikipedia, "Dysrationalia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dysrationalia); Stanovich, K. E. (1994). "Reconceptualizing intelligence: Dysrationalia as an intuition pump." *Educational Researcher*, 23(4) — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0013189X023004011)
- *What Intelligence Tests Miss: The Psychology of Rational Thought* (Yale University Press). Sources disagree on the year: Wikipedia says 2009, while Stanovich's *Scientific American* essay refers to "his 2010 book" (probably the paperback) — [Wikipedia, "Keith Stanovich"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Stanovich); [Scientific American, "Rational and Irrational Thought: The Thinking That IQ Tests Miss"](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/rational-and-irrational-thought-the-thinking-that-iq-tests-miss/)
- Stanovich, West & Toplak (2016), *The Rationality Quotient: Toward a Test of Rational Thinking* (MIT Press), introduces the Comprehensive Assessment of Rational Thinking (CART). The publisher's framing: standard IQ tests do not measure "any of the broad components of rationality — adaptive responding, good judgment, and good decision making" — [MIT Press interview](https://mitpress.mit.edu/five-minutes-with-keith-e-stanovich-richard-f-west-and-maggie-e-toplak/); [Stanovich 2016 Thorndike Award address, "The Comprehensive Assessment of Rational Thinking," *Educational Psychologist*](http://www.keithstanovich.com/Site/Research_on_Reasoning_files/Stanovich_EdPsy_2016.pdf); [Frontiers book review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6707212/)

**How strongly the CART relates to intelligence** (Stanovich, Toplak & West, 2020, "Intelligence and Rationality," *Cambridge Handbook of Intelligence*, 2nd ed.) — [author PDF](http://keithstanovich.com/Site/Research_on_Reasoning_files/Stanovich_Toplak_West_2020.pdf)
- In more than 700 participants, the CART total correlated about 0.50–0.70 with various cognitive-ability measures.
- Subtest correlations varied a lot: reflective-thinking subtests .51 and .54; avoiding overconfidence .38; argument evaluation .37; optimal temporal discounting .06.
- Actively open-minded thinking correlated significantly more strongly with CART performance than with cognitive ability. It also predicted almost every CART subtest after cognitive ability was partialled out.

**Myside bias and other biases**
- Stanovich, West & Toplak (2013), "Myside bias, rational thinking, and intelligence" (*Current Directions in Psychological Science*): the magnitude of myside bias "shows very little relation to intelligence" — [PDF](https://iphils.uj.edu.pl/~a.trybus/classes/logic/cs/12.Stanovich.Myside.Bias.pdf)
- In two experiments with more than 1,400 university students and eight comparisons, there was "very little evidence" that higher-ability participants showed less natural myside bias. The secondary summary attributes this to Stanovich & West (2007, *Thinking & Reasoning*). Macpherson & Stanovich (2007) found near-zero correlations between cognitive ability and myside bias in two paradigms (secondary) — [Global Cognition summary](https://www.globalcognition.org/intelligence-and-cognitive-bias/)
- Stanovich & West (2008), "On the relative independence of thinking biases and cognitive ability" (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*), is reported to show that a number of thinking biases, including myside bias, are uncorrelated with cognitive ability (secondary) — [Global Cognition](https://www.globalcognition.org/intelligence-and-cognitive-bias/). Caution: a critical reading notes that West et al. state that most cognitive biases are negatively correlated with cognitive ability (smarter people show somewhat fewer of them). The independence finding applies to specific biases, not all — [LessWrong discussion](https://www.lesswrong.com/lw/d1u/the_new_yorker_article_on_cognitive_biases)

**Bias blind spot (West, Meserve & Stanovich, 2012)**
- West, Meserve & Stanovich (2012). "Cognitive sophistication does not attenuate the bias blind spot." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 103(3), 506–519.
  - Bias blind spots replicated across many classic heuristics-and-biases tasks, and none were attenuated by cognitive ability or bias-related thinking dispositions.
  - A larger blind spot was associated with higher cognitive ability.
  - Being free of the blind spot did not predict avoiding the actual biases.
  - Sources: [JDM 2022 follow-up discussing West et al.](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/22/220208/jdm220208.html); [abstract copy](https://scrapbox.io/nishio-en/Cognitive_sophistication_does_not_attenuate_the_bias_blind_spot)
- A 2022 *Judgment and Decision Making* paper notes that a later study (Scopelliti et al., 2015) used social/motivational items rather than cognitive-bias items, so the domains differ — [JDM 2022](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/22/220208/jdm220208.html)

**Cognitive Reflection Test (Frederick, 2005)**
- Frederick, S. (2005). "Cognitive reflection and decision making." *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 19(4), 25–42 — [PDF](http://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/frederick-jep2005.pdf)
  - Bat-and-ball item: the bat and ball cost $1.10 in total and the bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. The intuitive answer is 10¢; the correct answer is 5¢.
- Frederick reported CRT correlations of .44 with SAT total and .43 with the Wonderlic, as cited in Toplak, West & Stanovich (2011), "The Cognitive Reflection Test as a predictor of performance on heuristics-and-biases tasks," *Memory & Cognition* — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-011-0104-1)
- Toplak et al. (2011) argue that the CRT captures "some additional ability or disposition, not shared with non-lure measures, to resist reporting a first response that comes to mind" — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-011-0104-1)

**Motivated numeracy (Kahan, Peters, Dawson & Slovic, 2017)**
- "Motivated numeracy and enlightened self-government." *Behavioural Public Policy* (online May 2017; working paper 2013) — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioural-public-policy/article/abs/motivated-numeracy-and-enlightened-selfgovernment/EC9F2410D5562EF10B7A5E2539063806); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2319992)
  - Highly numerate participants solved a neutral covariance problem (skin-rash cream) better.
  - On an identical gun-control version, answers became polarized, and polarization was greatest among the most numerate. The authors read this as quantitative skill being used selectively to protect identity.
- A Western European replication with an active-reasoning intervention: Connor et al. (2020), *Behavioural Public Policy* — [TU/e record](https://research.tue.nl/nl/publications/motivated-numeracy-and-active-reasoning-in-a-western-european-sam/)

**Epistemically suspect beliefs**
- A 2019 study, "'Dysrationalia' among university students," looks at how cognitive ability, components of rational thought, and self-control each explain epistemically suspect beliefs — [PMC6396694](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6396694/). Details not extracted.

### Inferences
- This is the strongest evidence for a third term the thesis can use: knowledge ≠ intelligence ≠ rationality. Stanovich's tripartite view separates the reflective mind (dispositions), the algorithmic mind (IQ) and "mindware" (learned rules such as probability and logic). Mindware is itself a kind of knowledge, which is relevant: some rational thinking requires specific learned knowledge, and IQ alone does not supply it.
- The bias-blind-spot and motivated-numeracy findings support a sharper thesis: more cognitive capacity or knowledge can strengthen rationalization rather than correct it.
- The .50–.70 CART–IQ correlation means the thesis should not claim that rationality is unrelated to intelligence. The defensible claim is that a substantial share of rational-thinking variance is not captured by IQ, and some components (temporal discounting, r ≈ .06) are nearly unrelated.

### Gaps
- I could not access *What Intelligence Tests Miss* directly for page-level quotations.
- Exact correlations from Stanovich & West (2008, JPSP) per bias task were not retrieved.
- I did not find a CART-specific Brazilian/Portuguese validation in this session.
- The common claim that "about 50% of Harvard/MIT/Princeton students answered the bat-and-ball item incorrectly" (Frederick's school-level CRT results) was not verified here. Check Table 1 of the Frederick (2005) PDF linked above.

## 4. The Flynn effect: abstract reasoning vs. knowledge, and the "negative Flynn effect"

### Takeaway
Twentieth-century IQ gains were concentrated in fluid and abstract-reasoning tests (Similarities, Raven-type matrices). Knowledge-based subtests (Vocabulary, Information, Arithmetic) gained very little: on the WISC, Similarities rose about 24 points versus about 2 for Information. A 2015 meta-analysis covering about 4 million people confirms that fluid gains (0.41 IQ points per year) outpaced crystallized gains (0.21 per year). Flynn read this as a culturally learned shift toward abstract, hypothetical classification ("scientific spectacles"), not as more knowledge or a rise in g. Since about the 1990s gains have slowed or reversed in several countries, with Norwegian within-family data showing the reversal is environmental.

### Cited Findings
**Pietschnig & Voracek (2015) meta-analysis**
- Pietschnig, J., & Voracek, M. (2015). "One century of global IQ gains: A formal meta-analysis of the Flynn effect (1909–2013)." *Perspectives on Psychological Science* — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1745691615577701); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/303125919_One_century_of_global_IQ_gains_A_formal_meta-analysis_of_the_Flynn_effect_1909-2013)
  - 271 independent samples, almost 4 million participants, 31 countries.
  - Annual gains: fluid IQ 0.41 points, spatial 0.30, full-scale 0.28, crystallized 0.21 (as summarized in [Wikipedia, "Flynn effect"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flynn_effect); verify in the article tables).
  - Gains had decreased in recent decades.

**Subtest pattern and Flynn's interpretation**
- Flynn, J. R. (2007). *What Is Intelligence? Beyond the Flynn Effect*. Cambridge University Press.
  - Per a reposted book excerpt, WISC Information gained only about 2 IQ points while Similarities gained about 24 (secondary repost; verify the figure in the book) — [Free Republic repost](https://freerepublic.com/focus/f-news/1907735/posts)
  - *Smithsonian* gives about 25 points over roughly 50 years on Similarities, with "extremely small" gains on arithmetic — [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/are-you-smarter-than-your-grandfather-probably-not-150402883/)
- The lowest gains, near zero per year, were on Wechsler verbal subtests such as Vocabulary, Information and Arithmetic — [Encyclopedia.com, "Flynn Effect"](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences/applied-and-social-sciences-magazines/flynn-effect); [EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/education/flynn-effect/)
- Reviews summarize Flynn's argument this way: the largest gains came on subtests measuring abstract reasoning and pattern recognition, while knowledge-dependent subtests rose least. This reflects "a shift in particular habits of mind" rather than higher general intelligence — [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/are-you-smarter-than-your-grandfather-probably-not-150402883/); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/will-we-keep-getting-smarter-flynn-effect-says-yes/)
- Illustrative example: today we say dogs and rabbits are both mammals, while a nineteenth-century American might have said "you use dogs to hunt rabbits." The earlier generations "did not participate in the twentieth century's great cognitive revolution, in which we learned to sort experience according to a new set of abstract categories" (Gladwell's *New Yorker* review of Flynn, quoted in a blog) — [Electrostani, quoting Gladwell 2007](https://www.electrostani.com/2007/12/gladwell-problems-with-iq-tests.html)
- **Critique:** Bryan Caplan argues the "scientific spectacles" account has a hole. Most American adults are scientifically illiterate, answering about 60% of basic true/false science questions correctly on the GSS, so a scientific ethos is a doubtful cause — [Econlib](https://www.econlib.org/are-we-getting-smarter-thoughts-on-the-flynn-effect/)
- Critical review of *What Is Intelligence?* by Cosma Shalizi — [bactra.org](https://bactra.org/reviews/flynn-beyond/)

**Negative (reverse) Flynn effect**
- Bratsberg, B., & Rogeberg, O. (2018). "Flynn effect and its reversal are both environmentally caused." *PNAS*, 115(26). DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1718793115 — [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1718793115); [PMC6042097](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6042097/); [Frisch Centre](https://frisch.uio.no/publikasjoner/?pubid=1372)
  - Data: Norwegian military conscription scores for birth cohorts 1962–1991, compared between brothers.
  - The rise, turning point and decline are all fully recovered within families. This rules out genetic or family-composition explanations (e.g., dysgenic fertility).
  - The specific environmental causes are not identified — [The Science Breaker](https://www.thesciencebreaker.org/breaks/psychology/norwegian-iq-scores-are-falling-but-genes-are-not-to-blame)
- Dworak, Revelle & Condon (2023). "Looking for Flynn effects in a recent online U.S. adult sample: Examining shifts within the SAPA Project." *Intelligence* — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0160289623000156)
  - A secondary blog reports declines between 2006 and 2018 on matrix reasoning and vocabulary, and lists 3D rotation among them — [cogn-iq.org](https://www.cogn-iq.org/blog/flynn-effect-reversal/). **Conflict:** my recollection is that 3D rotation (spatial) scores rose in that paper. Verify before citing.

### Inferences
- The Flynn pattern supports the thesis directly. A population can gain large amounts on reasoning tests within one or two generations with almost no gain in vocabulary, information or arithmetic, so reasoning scores and knowledge scores are separable at the population level.
- It also cuts the other way. Flynn's own explanation is that abstract reasoning habits were learned from schooling and modern culture. What IQ tests call "fluid" reasoning is partly culturally acquired mindware, a kind of knowledge about how to think. The Gf/Gc line is therefore not purely biological capacity vs. learned content.
- The reverse Flynn effect being environmental, within families, reinforces that measured "intelligence" is sensitive to the informational and educational environment.

### Gaps
- The exact 24-point Similarities vs. 2-point Information figures come from a reposted excerpt. Confirm the table in Flynn (2007), ch. 1–2, before quoting.
- I could not confirm whether the phrase "scientific spectacles" appears verbatim in Flynn (2007), in *Are We Getting Smarter?* (2012), or only in his TED talk and interviews. Search results did not surface Flynn's own wording.
- The magnitude of the Norwegian decline (often quoted as about 7 points per generation) was not verified. One blog's description of the data (1970–2009 testers) conflicts with the paper's 1962–1991 birth cohorts.
- Teasdale & Owen (2005, Denmark), Dutton, van der Linden & Lynn (2016) and other negative-Flynn studies were not retrieved this session.
- No Brazil-specific Flynn-effect data were retrieved.

## 5. Sternberg (practical intelligence / tacit knowledge), Gardner, savants, and high reasoning with little schooling (Ceci & Liker; Carraher, Carraher & Schliemann)

### Takeaway
Several lines of evidence break the link between academic or schooled knowledge and real-world reasoning:
- Kenyan children's practical knowledge of herbal medicines correlated trivially or negatively with academic intelligence.
- Expert racetrack handicappers with some low IQs used complex multivariate reasoning, and IQ did not predict their success.
- Brazilian street-vendor children in Recife solved arithmetic correctly while selling, then failed the same problems in school format (e.g., 40 × 3 = 70 on paper).
- Savants show the reverse dissociation: huge memorized knowledge (Kim Peek, about 9,000 books) alongside severe limits in everyday reasoning and adaptation.

Gardner's "multiple intelligences" offers weaker support, because empirical tests find that most purely cognitive MI measures load on g.

### Cited Findings
**Sternberg: practical intelligence and tacit knowledge (Kenya)**
- Sternberg, R. J., Nokes, C., Geissler, P. W., Prince, R., Okatcha, F., Bundy, D. A., & Grigorenko, E. L. (2001). "The relationship between academic and practical intelligence: A case study in Kenya." *Intelligence*, 29(5) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0160289601000654); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/223473563_The_Relationship_between_Academic_and_Practical_Intelligence_A_Case_Study_in_Kenya)
  - 85 children aged 12–15 in a rural village in western Kenya, where more than 95% of children suffered from parasitic illnesses.
  - Children took a test of tacit knowledge of natural herbal medicines used against illness. Scores "correlated trivially or significantly negatively with measures of academic intelligence and achievement, even after controlling for socioeconomic status."
- A National Academies chapter describes a similar design, attributed to Sternberg & Grigorenko (1997b), with a negative correlation to a formal knowledge-based test. It may be the same dataset — [NAP, *Minority Students in Special and Gifted Education*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10128/chapter/10)
- Sternberg, R. J. (2007). "Who are the bright children? The cultural context of being and acting intelligent." *Educational Researcher* — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/0013189x07299881)

**Ceci & Liker: racetrack handicappers**
- Ceci, S. J., & Liker, J. K. (1986). "A day at the races: A study of IQ, expertise, and cognitive complexity." *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 115(3) — [UNLV library record](https://special.library.unlv.edu/node/282229)

**Carraher, Carraher & Schliemann: street mathematics (Brazil)**
- Carraher, T. N., Carraher, D. W., & Schliemann, A. D. (1985). "Mathematics in the streets and in schools." *British Journal of Developmental Psychology*, 3(1), 21–29. DOI: 10.1111/j.2044-835X.1985.tb00951.x — [Wiley](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.2044-835X.1985.tb00951.x); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232587227_Mathematics_in_the_Streets_and_in_Schools)

  - India: Banerjee et al. (2017), "The untapped math skills of working children in India" — [J-PAL PDF](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/WP4800_Untapped-Math-Skills-of-Children-in-India_Banerjee-et-al_Aug2017.pdf)

**Gardner's multiple intelligences: empirical test**
- Visser, B. A., Ashton, M. C., & Vernon, P. A. (2006). "Beyond g: Putting multiple intelligences theory to the test." *Intelligence*, 34(5) — [Brock University repository](https://brocku.scholaris.ca/items/73c8269c-91f8-4fd3-9d52-32b4fce2b89a)
  - Two measures for each of Gardner's eight domains were given to 200 participants.
  - There was a positive manifold and a large g factor. All purely cognitive tests correlated significantly with general ability; musical, bodily-kinesthetic and one intrapersonal measure did not.
  - Gardner replied ("On failing to grasp the core of MI theory," 2006) that the measures were biased toward linguistic and logical formats — [Riot IQ summary (secondary)](https://www.riotiq.com/articles/advanced-topics-and-research/the-g-factor-vs-gardner-s-multiple-intelligences-what-the-evidence-actually-shows)

### Inferences
- The Recife street-vendor study is ideal for a Brazilian audience. The children had reasoning competence (correct, flexible mental calculation under real conditions) without the school knowledge (written algorithms), and school knowledge failed to show the competence they had. It is a double blow to equating "knowing school content" with "being intelligent."
- Precision note: Carraher et al. is not an IQ study. It shows that competence depends on context and representation (oral and meaningful vs. written and symbolic). It supports "school knowledge ≠ reasoning ability" more than a claim about psychometric g. Ceci & Liker is the IQ-based complement: complex reasoning was not predicted by IQ.
- Savants show the reverse dissociation: massive stored information or narrow skill coexisting with poor general adaptive reasoning. This is a vivid illustration that knowledge alone is not intelligence. Caution: savants are rare and neurologically atypical, so the cases are illustrative rather than proof for typical populations.
- Gardner is weak support for the thesis. Most MI measures that are actually cognitive load on g, so cite him only briefly and with the Visser et al. caveat.

### Gaps
- **Carraher et al. (1985) percent-correct figures were not confirmed this session.** From memory: about 98% correct on informal (sales) problems vs. about 74% on word problems vs. about 37% on context-free computation, across about 60+ problems. Verify in the BJDP article (DOI above) or in the 1982 *Cadernos de Pesquisa* PDF before quoting.
- **Ceci & Liker (1986) IQ statistics were not confirmed.** From memory: expert IQs ranged from about 81 to about 128, the IQ–handicapping-accuracy correlation was about −.07, and a low-IQ expert used a seven-variable interactive model. There is also a published critique: Detterman & Spry (1988), "Is it smart to play the horses?", *JEP: General*. Verify.
- Sternberg's triarchic / successful-intelligence theory (analytical, creative, practical) and the Wagner & Sternberg tacit-knowledge inventories for managers were not specifically retrieved. Nor was the critique by Gottfredson (2003), "Dissecting practical intelligence theory," *Intelligence* 31, which argues tacit-knowledge tests are job knowledge and correlate with g.
- I found no reliable IQ-range statistics for savants this session.

## 6. Counter-evidence: where the knowledge/intelligence distinction breaks down

### Takeaway
The distinction is real but not clean:
1. Gc is highly g-loaded, and in CHC modeling the Gc factor collapses into verbal comprehension while Gf and g become statistically indistinguishable (Kan et al., 2011).
2. Investment theory holds that Gc is produced by investing Gf in learning, and in personnel psychology general ability predicts job performance mainly through faster acquisition of job knowledge (Schmidt & Hunter).
3. Schooling, the main transmitter of knowledge, causally raises IQ scores by about 1–5 points per year, roughly 3.4 on average.
4. Gc correlates with chess skill, CRT scores correlate .43–.44 with SAT/IQ, and CART correlates .50–.70 with ability.

Knowledge and reasoning are separable in measurement, age trajectory and transfer, but they feed each other causally.

### Cited Findings
**The status of Gc in CHC (Kan et al., 2011)**
- Kan, K.-J., Kievit, R. A., Dolan, C., & van der Maas, H. (2011). "On the interpretation of the CHC factor Gc." *Intelligence*, 39(5), 292–302. DOI: 10.1016/j.intell.2011.05.003 — [VU Amsterdam](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/on-the-interpretation-of-the-chc-factor-gc/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221705052_On_the_interpretation_of_the_CHC_factor_Gc); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/On-the-Interpretation-of-the-CHC-Factor-Gc.-Kan-Kievit/fbb5be260180a53b3068659c2f3c0a412889a3ff)
  - Conceptual argument: if CHC Gc is a capacity, it cannot be "crystallized intelligence" in Cattell's sense; if it is crystallized intelligence, it is not a capacity. Under investment theory, Gf and g "represent one and the same capacity."
  - In a CFA of Human Cognitive Abilities project data, Gf and g were statistically indistinguishable and Gc was statistically equivalent to verbal comprehension. Gc and g could be removed without loss of fit.
- Later work reads this as suggesting that CHC Gc may amount to verbal ability, leaving out knowledge in other domains. The same later paper notes that investment theory is only weakly evidenced — [ICA Journal, "More than 'just 1 g'? The general intelligence paradox and its solution"](https://icajournal.scholasticahq.com/article/155379-more-than-just-1-_-g_-the-general-intelligence-paradox-and-its-solution)
- Schipolowski, Wilhelm & Schroeders (2014). "On the nature of crystallized intelligence: The relationship between verbal ability and factual knowledge." *Intelligence* — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0160289614000865). Details not extracted.

**Intelligence → knowledge → performance (personnel psychology)**
- Schmidt, F. L., & Hunter, J. (2004). "General mental ability in the world of work: Occupational attainment and job performance." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 86(1), 162–173 — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/8922930_General_Mental_Ability_in_the_World_of_Work_Occupational_Attainment_and_Job_Performance)
  - GMA predicts both the occupational level people reach and their performance within it, better than other abilities, traits or job experience.
  - The causal model is GMA → job knowledge → job performance: GMA's main direct effect is on how fast job knowledge is acquired — [Semantic Scholar figure](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/General-mental-ability-in-the-world-of-work:-and-Schmidt-Hunter/a957c2ecafe00ad83c48efaf783e07c9d276ea88/figure/2); [cogn-iq.org summary](https://www.cogn-iq.org/blog/cognitive-tests-job-performance/)
- Schmidt & Hunter (1998), *Psychological Bulletin*: GMA is the most valid predictor of future performance and learning when hiring people without prior job experience — [Scribd copy](https://www.scribd.com/document/66407853/Hunter-and-Schmidt-1998-The-Validity-and-Utility-of-Selection-Methods-in-Personnel-Psychology-Practical-and-Theoretical-Implications-of-85-Years-of-Research-Findings). Its classic validity estimate (r = .51) was revised downward by Sackett et al. (2022); the secondary source does not give the revised value — [cogn-iq.org](https://www.cogn-iq.org/blog/cognitive-tests-job-performance/)
- Related: Schmidt, Hunter & Outerbridge (1986), "Impact of job experience and ability on job knowledge, work sample performance, and supervisory ratings of job performance" — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232601470_Impact_of_Job_Experience_and_Ability_on_Job_Knowledge_Work_Sample_Performance_and_Supervisory_Ratings_of_Job_Performance)

**Education causally raises IQ (Ritchie & Tucker-Drob, 2018)**
- Ritchie, S. J., & Tucker-Drob, E. M. (2018). "How much does education improve intelligence? A meta-analysis." *Psychological Science* — [author PDF](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/tucker-drob/files/2019/08/Ritchie-Tucker-Drob-2018-Psych-Science-How-Much-Does-Education-Improve-Intelligence.pdf); [Edinburgh Research Explorer](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/how-much-does-education-improve-intelligence-a-meta-analysis/)

### Inferences
- The strongest objection to "conhecimento ≠ inteligência" is causal entanglement, not the correlation alone. Intelligence speeds the acquisition of knowledge (investment theory; Schmidt & Hunter's path model). Knowledge-transmitting institutions (schooling) raise measured intelligence (Ritchie & Tucker-Drob; Flynn). And reasoning well often needs specific learned "mindware" (Stanovich).
- A defensible thesis is therefore: knowledge and intelligence are distinct constructs that are measured differently, age differently, and transfer differently, but they are causally intertwined, and neither can be reduced to the other. Some convenient claims overreach and should be avoided: that they are "independent," that "IQ doesn't matter for expertise," or that "knowledge is irrelevant to intelligence."
- Kan et al. (2011) can also be used for the thesis. If the CHC "Gc" factor is really just verbal comprehension, then IQ batteries capture "knowledge" only narrowly (mostly vocabulary and verbal information). Most domain knowledge, Ackerman's "dark matter," lies outside what IQ tests measure.
- Ritchie & Tucker-Drob leave open whether education raises g itself or only specific test-relevant skills. The thesis should not claim that schooling makes people more intelligent in a deep sense without that caveat.

- I did not find typical g-loadings for Gc subtests (vocabulary and information are often reported among the most g-loaded Wechsler subtests). This is unverified here.
- Ritchie & Tucker-Drob's discussion of whether gains are on g or on specific skills was not extracted.
