## Q1. Classical distinctions: Heraclitus, Socrates, Plato (Meno, Theaetetus), Aristotle (NE VI), Montaigne

### Takeaway
The distinction between *having many items of knowledge* and *being intelligent, understanding or wise* is ancient and explicit. Heraclitus B40 says polymathy does not teach *noos*. Socrates says craftsmen with real knowledge still lack wisdom. Plato's Meno says true opinions are worthless until "tied down" by reasoning about causes. Aristotle separates five intellectual states and notes that the young can master mathematics but not practical wisdom, which requires experience of particulars. Montaigne prefers "une tête bien faite" to "une tête bien pleine".

### Inferences
- The classical sources support three distinct contrasts, which the thesis should keep apart:
  - accumulation vs grasp of unity or causes (Heraclitus, *Meno* 98a);
  - theoretical knowledge vs practical judgement about particulars (Aristotle's *epistēmē*/*sophia* vs *phronēsis*);
  - knowledge vs awareness of its limits (Socrates).
  "Intelligence" in the thesis can map onto *noos*/*nous*, onto *phronēsis*, or onto the Platonic "tether". The thesis should say which.
- Caution: in Plato and Aristotle, *epistēmē* in the strict sense already includes explanatory grasp. So "conhecimento ≠ inteligência" is cleanest when "conhecimento" means *information, true belief or polymathy*, not *epistēmē* in the Platonic sense.
- Aristotle's young-mathematician remark shows that skill with abstract knowledge and practical intelligence can come apart in the same person, which is good evidence for the thesis.

## Q2. Ryle (knowing-how vs knowing-that), Polanyi (tacit knowledge), Dreyfus (critique of symbolic AI, skill acquisition)

### Takeaway
Ryle (1949) is the central modern source. He argues that intelligence is shown in *how* one acts (competences and dispositions), not in a stock of known propositions. The "intellectualist legend", which says every intelligent act is preceded by consulting a proposition, generates a vicious regress. Polanyi adds that much of what we know cannot be articulated at all. Dreyfus turns both points against symbolic AI: expertise is not rule-following over stored facts.

### Cited Findings

**Gilbert Ryle, *The Concept of Mind* (London: Hutchinson, 1949), ch. II, "Knowing How and Knowing That"**
- SEP "Knowledge How" summarises Ryle's challenge to the view that knowing how to do something reduces to knowing that certain propositions are true. Ryle held that they are distinct kinds of knowledge. — [SEP, "Knowledge How"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/knowledge-how/) (mirror: [SEOP/ILLC](https://seop.illc.uva.nl/entries/knowledge-how))
- The regress argument (as presented in the SEP entry, citing Ryle 1949: 19–20 in the pagination SEP uses): applying a proposition is itself something that can be done intelligently or stupidly. If every intelligent act required a prior intelligent theoretical act, no one could ever begin acting intelligently. — [SEP, "Knowledge How"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/knowledge-how/)
- Ryle's conclusion: "'Intelligent' cannot be defined in terms of 'intellectual' or 'knowing how' in terms of 'knowing that'; 'thinking what I am doing' does not connote 'both thinking what to do and doing it'. ### Inferences
- Ryle gives the thesis its sharpest formulation. Intelligence words ("clever", "stupid") ascribe *abilities* and *dispositions*, not stocks of truths. Ignorance is often only a *symptom* of stupidity, so the two differ in logical category, not just in degree.
- Ryle also concedes that knowledge is a precondition (the surgeon line). The best version of the thesis is therefore "knowledge is necessary but not sufficient", not "knowledge is irrelevant".
- Polanyi and Dreyfus add a further point. The most expert intelligence may be the *least* reducible to statable knowledge, so a store of explicit propositions, however large, cannot capture it. This bears directly on the AI debate (Q4).

## Q3. Knowledge vs understanding in contemporary epistemology (Kvanvig, Zagzebski, Pritchard, Elgin, Grimm)

### Takeaway
Since Kvanvig (2003), a major strand of epistemology argues that *understanding*, the grasp of how pieces of information hang together and explain one another, is distinct from propositional knowledge. On these views it is also more valuable. Pritchard argues that understanding is a cognitive achievement and that the two "come apart" in both directions. Elgin goes further: understanding can rest on felicitous falsehoods (models, idealisations), so it is not even strictly factive.

### Cited Findings
- **Jonathan Kvanvig, *The Value of Knowledge and the Pursuit of Understanding* (Cambridge University Press, 2003).** He argues that understanding, rather than knowledge, is the distinctively valuable epistemic standing. As Pritchard summarises it, Kvanvig holds that "it is understanding, not knowledge, which is distinctively valuable." — [Pritchard, "Knowledge, Understanding and Epistemic Value", *Royal Institute of Philosophy Supplement* (Cambridge, 2009)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/royal-institute-of-philosophy-supplements/article/knowledge-understanding-and-epistemic-value/4FC236E1E680B15A00AE854F1693F920); [Academia.edu, "Knowledge and Understanding"](https://www.academia.edu/19857416/Knowledge_and_Understanding)
- Kvanvig's main case is *objectual* understanding: understanding a whole subject matter, e.g. "the Comanche dominance of the southern plains". He argued that understanding, unlike knowledge, is compatible with epistemic luck. For example, someone whose true beliefs come from a book of largely accurate history, where the accuracy was a matter of luck, may understand without knowing. — [Wilkenfeld, Plunkett & Lombrozo, "Folk attributions of understanding: is there a role for epistemic luck?", *Episteme* (2018)](https://cognition.princeton.edu/publications/folk-attributions-understanding-there-role-epistemic-luck); [Cambridge Core, *Episteme*](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/episteme/article/folk-attributions-of-understanding-is-there-a-role-for-epistemic-luck/23040B133FA56C6EF1DD5195DF2E07E5)
- Contested: Stephen Grimm (2006) responded with a case meant to show that lucky understanding is impossible. Rohwer (2014, "Lucky understanding without knowledge") sides with Kvanvig. Wilkenfeld et al. (2018) found folk intuitions that "challenge Kvanvig's position". — [Princeton cognition lab](https://cognition.princeton.edu/publications/folk-attributions-understanding-there-role-epistemic-luck); [PhilPapers, Rohwer](https://api.philpapers.org/rec/ROHLUW); [Cambridge, *Episteme*, "Understanding, grasping and luck"](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/episteme/article/abs/understanding-grasping-and-luck/84D58F345E73D83606DE97C4DE955A12)
- **Duncan Pritchard** (in Pritchard, Millar & Haddock, *The Nature and Value of Knowledge: Three Investigations*, Oxford UP, 2010) argues that "understanding and knowledge come apart, and come apart precisely because understanding, unlike knowledge, is a form of cognitive achievement, and hence is finally valuable." - This literature gives the thesis a respectable contemporary vocabulary. "Conhecimento" maps onto *propositional knowledge* (knowing that p). - Elgin's non-factivity implies that someone can be *more* intelligent or understanding while holding *some false* beliefs (idealisations). That is a striking way to show that intelligence is not measured by the number of true propositions held.

## Q4. Searle's Chinese Room, Block's "Blockhead", and the Turing test

### Takeaway
Two thought experiments show that producing all the right outputs does not suffice for understanding or intelligence. Searle's Chinese Room says symbol manipulation by rule (syntax) does not yield meaning (semantics). Block's Blockhead imagines a machine that passes any Turing test by looking up pre-stored responses in a giant table, and is intuitively not intelligent. Block's conclusion, "psychologism", says that whether behaviour is intelligent depends on the internal processing that produces it. That is the cleanest philosophical argument that *storing* the right answers is not *intelligence*.

### Cited Findings

**John Searle, "Minds, Brains, and Programs", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 3(3) (1980): 417–424 (target article; 417–457 with 27 peer commentaries and reply)**

The paper is aimed specifically at Schank & Abelson's (1977) story-understanding programs. — [Univ. of Birmingham, *Computers and Thought* ch. 10](https://poplog.fricas.org/bham/computers-and-thought/chap10/node5.html); [W. Rapaport (Buffalo), "Chinese Room Argument"](https://cse.buffalo.edu/~rapaport/572/S02/searle.html)
- Searle, locked in the room and following an English rulebook to manipulate Chinese symbols, produces answers indistinguishable from a native speaker's yet "[doesn't] understand a word of Chinese." [VERIFIED-SNIPPET (wording variants)] — [Birmingham](https://poplog.fricas.org/bham/computers-and-thought/chap10/node5.html). Standard text (1980: 418): "It seems to me quite obvious in the example that I do not understand a word of the Chinese stories. I have inputs and outputs that are indistinguishable from those of the native Chinese speaker, and I can have any formal program you like, but I still understand nothing." **Ned Block, "Psychologism and Behaviorism", *The Philosophical Review* 90(1) (1981): 5–43**
- Psychologism: "the doctrine that whether behavior is intelligent behavior depends on the character of the internal information processing that produces it." [VERIFIED-SNIPPET, close paraphrase of abstract] — [PhilPapers, BLOPAB](https://philpapers.org/rec/BLOPAB); [full text hosted at nedblock.us](https://www.nedblock.us/papers/Psychologism.htm)

- Precursor: Claude Shannon and John McCarthy proposed a similar lookup-table objection to behavioural definitions of thinking in 1956 (Preface to *Automata Studies*). — [Wikipedia, "Blockhead"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockhead_(thought_experiment)); [Wikipedia, "Ned Block"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ned_Block)
- Block's target is the "neo-Turing test conception of intelligence": intelligence (conversational intelligence) as "the capacity to produce a sensible sequence of verbal responses to a sequence of verbal stimuli, whatever they may be." [UNVERIFIED: wording from memory of Block 1981] — [nedblock.us](https://www.nedblock.us/papers/Psychologism.htm)
- Objections:
  - Such a table is physically impossible because of combinatorial explosion.
  - A long enough test could expose a table limited to finite histories.
  - Hanoch Ben-Yami (2005) argues that Block's example actually supports a form of behaviourism.
  — [Wikipedia, "Blockhead"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockhead_(thought_experiment)); [CEU Research Portal, Ben-Yami 2005](https://research.ceu.edu/en/publications/behaviorism-and-psychologism-why-blocks-argument-against-behavior/); [PhilPapers, BENBAP](https://philpapers.org/rec/BENBAP)
- Peter Millican's Oxford Turing lectures (2018) note that "we're not thinking of intelligence as merely to be measured by the appropriate behaviour", which is the intuition Block's case relies on. — [Oxford podcast transcript, Millican, Turing 2018 lecture 7](https://media.podcasts.ox.ac.uk/philfac/2018_millican_turing/Turing_2018_TT_7.srt)
- Turing test: Alan Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence", *Mind* 59(236) (1950): 433–460. It replaces "Can machines think?" with the imitation game, a behavioural criterion. [Bibliographic details UNVERIFIED in this session] — context in [Wikipedia, "Blockhead"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockhead_(thought_experiment))

### Inferences
- Blockhead is the most direct philosophical model of "knowledge ≠ intelligence". The machine *contains* every right answer (maximal stored "knowledge" in the sense of information) and is not intelligent. What is missing is the generative, general capacity to produce answers it was never given. In Block's words, intelligence depends on *how* outputs are produced, not merely *that* they are produced.
- Searle's argument is about *understanding/meaning*, not intelligence as such. The thesis should not conflate the two. One can accept Block (intelligence requires the right internal processing) while rejecting Searle (who holds that no program suffices for understanding).
- Application to large language models: whether LLMs are "Blockhead-like" (memorised lookup) or generalise is an empirical question, and the thought experiment does not settle it. The thesis should present Blockhead as a conceptual criterion, not as a verdict on current AI. (Inference; no 2025–2026 sources on LLM-Blockhead debates were retrieved.)

## Q5. Whitehead ("inert ideas"), Dewey (reflective thinking), Kant (judgement as an unteachable talent; "stupidity")

Rules can be taught to the understanding, but *judgement*, the capacity to apply rules to cases, is a talent that can only be exercised, not taught. ## Q7. Counterarguments: intelligence as a kind of knowledge (intellectualism) and the necessity of knowledge for reasoning

### Takeaway
The strongest opposition comes from intellectualism about know-how. Stanley & Williamson (2001) argue that knowing how is "simply a species of knowing that". If so, skilled and intelligent action just is a kind of propositional knowledge, and the Rylean wedge collapses. A weaker, widely shared counterpoint holds that intelligence cannot operate without knowledge (Ryle's surgeon, Aristotle's universals plus particulars). Contemporary AI theory (Chollet 2019) takes a middle path: priors and experience (knowledge) can "buy" skill, which is why skill is not intelligence. Intelligence is the efficiency of turning limited knowledge into new skill.

### Cited Findings
- **Jason Stanley & Timothy Williamson, "Knowing How", *The Journal of Philosophy* 98(8) (2001): 411–444.** They reject the fundamental distinction between "knowing-how" and "knowing-that". ### Inferences
- The thesis should face intellectualism directly. Even if Stanley & Williamson are right that know-how is propositional knowledge, the knowledge in question is of a special kind: knowledge of *ways to act*, held under a *practical mode of presentation*. It is not the kind of fact-storage the thesis means by "conhecimento". Intellectualism can then be granted at the level of logical form while the thesis is kept at the level of *encyclopaedic or informational* knowledge.
- Chollet's framing supports a defensible "weak" version of the thesis. Knowledge (priors plus experience) is an *input* that can substitute for intelligence on any fixed task, which is exactly why performance on a task with a large knowledge base is not evidence of intelligence. This matches Blockhead (Q4) and Kant's "learned but stupid" person (Q5).
- The most honest formulation the literature supports: *knowledge is necessary but not sufficient for intelligence. Intelligence (judgement, understanding, know-how) is the capacity to apply, connect and extend knowledge to new particulars, and it can vary independently of the amount of knowledge possessed.*
